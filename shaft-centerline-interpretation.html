How to Interpret Average Shaft Centerline Position

Average shaft centerline (ASC) data describes the mean radial position of a rotor journal relative to a defined coordinate system. Its diagnostic value comes from a sound reference, verified probe geometry, bearing-clearance context and a clear understanding of what DC position can—and cannot—tell you.

Start with the measurement domain

Average shaft centerline analysis uses the DC component of proximity-probe signals to assess mean shaft position. It is different from an orbit plot, which represents dynamic shaft motion using AC vibration signals. Neither view should be substituted for the other.

1. Establish what the reference represents

A recorded rest or reference condition is the baseline for the comparison. It may represent a documented machine state before operation or another mechanically meaningful condition selected by the engineer. Preserve the actual measured reference voltages and the conditions under which they were recorded. Do not silently replace that reference with the bearing centre, a nominal voltage, or an assumed six-o'clock location.

When comparing multiple operating points, make sure the reference is consistent. If the reference changes, document why and treat the resulting position series accordingly; otherwise, an apparent shift may be caused by a changed baseline rather than a changed rotor position.

2. Verify probe calibration, polarity and angular orientation

Each channel must use the sensitivity and polarity applicable to its installed probe, extension cable, driver and shaft target. The two probe angles must be defined in one consistent machine coordinate convention. Confirm whether angles are measured clockwise or counter-clockwise and from which physical direction the machine is viewed.

Convert each channel's operating-to-reference voltage difference into a displacement using its calibrated sensitivity and polarity. Resolve the displacement along the installed probe direction, then combine the channel contributions in the chosen X-Y coordinate system. A sign or viewing-direction error can rotate or reverse the resulting vector, so check the calculated direction against known installation geometry before interpreting it.

3. Read position and clearance together

The bearing centre and the measured mechanical reference are not necessarily the same point. A position plot should preserve the chosen origin and show the entered bearing-clearance boundary in the same coordinate system. Circular clearance is a simplified radial model; an elliptical model may be more representative when the bearing design or measured geometry supports it.

If a calculated point lies outside the entered boundary, the direct conclusion is that the point is outside the selected geometric clearance model. The radial exceedance is the distance beyond that boundary under the model's assumptions. This finding warrants engineering review, but it does not by itself prove a specific failure mechanism or replace physical inspection.

4. Interpret trends in operating context

  • Load and process changes: Changes in radial load, process conditions or operating point may move the equilibrium position.
  • Thermal effects: Warm-up, differential expansion and changing oil temperature can affect clearances and measured position.
  • Lubrication: Oil viscosity, supply temperature, pressure and flow are relevant context, but DC position alone does not measure minimum film thickness.
  • Machine configuration: Probe mounting, bearing geometry, alignment and support conditions affect how the measurement should be understood.
  • Transient events: Start-up, shutdown, trips and load changes should be assessed with time-synchronised operating and dynamic data where available.

5. Avoid conclusions that DC position cannot support alone

DC shaft position does not independently quantify vibration severity, identify oil whirl, confirm rubbing, measure minimum oil-film thickness or establish babbitt condition. Those assessments require additional evidence such as AC vibration trends, spectra, orbit or waveform data, process variables, temperatures, lubrication information and inspection results.

Practical reporting rule

State the reference condition, probe sensitivity and polarity, installed probe angles, coordinate convention, bearing-clearance model, operating state and the measured result. Separate the geometric observation from the diagnostic hypothesis and state what additional evidence is needed to confirm the hypothesis.

Suggested field review sequence

  1. Confirm channel identity, calibration, polarity and probe angle.
  2. Validate the recorded rest/reference condition.
  3. Check operating readings and machine-state records.
  4. Calculate the relative position in a consistent coordinate system.
  5. Compare the point with the entered circular or elliptical clearance geometry.
  6. Review trends and corroborating vibration, process, temperature and inspection evidence.
  7. Report observations, limitations and recommended follow-up separately.

Frequently asked questions

What does it mean if a calculated shaft position is outside the entered clearance boundary?

It means the calculated point lies outside the selected clearance geometry under the entered reference, dimensions and coordinate assumptions. Verify the inputs and measurement convention, then investigate; the geometric result alone does not identify a failure mechanism.

Can average shaft centerline identify oil whirl or rubbing by itself?

No. DC position describes mean shaft location. Diagnosing oil whirl, rubbing or other dynamic faults requires corroborating evidence such as AC vibration, spectra, waveforms, operating data and inspection.

Should the bearing centre replace the recorded rest reference?

No. The confirmed mechanical rest/reference condition is the baseline for the relative position calculation. The bearing centre is a separate geometric reference and should not be substituted without an explicitly defined coordinate transformation.

What should be documented in a shaft-centerline report?

Document the reference condition, probe calibration and polarity, installed probe angles, coordinate convention, bearing-clearance model, machine operating state, calculated position and limitations of the interpretation.
